Mi­li­taire Spec­ta­tor, three De­fence his­to­ri­ans call for a new debate, 5 July 2025

The passage

“The narrow interpretation that got Dutchbat into trouble at the time was not inevitable; it was the product of a wider culture of restraint, of bureaucratic logic, and of the shifting (or wanting to shift) of responsibility, or the unwillingness to take it. Today, learning from that insight is more relevant than ever.” Second and third sentences of the closing section, ‘Intellectuele moed’ (Intellectual courage). Translated from the Dutch; the original wording is below.

Original wording

“De smalle interpretatie die Dutchbat destijds in problemen bracht, was niet onvermijdelijk; ze was het product van een bredere cultuur van terughoudendheid, van bureaucratische logica en van het (willen) afschuiven, of niet willen nemen, van verantwoordelijkheid. Vandaag de dag is leren van dat inzicht relevanter dan ooit.” The parentheses around ‘willen’ are the authors’ own. The construction, literally ‘the (wanting to) shifting away of responsibility, or not wanting to take it’, has no neat English equivalent and is rendered above as two alternatives.

Why this document matters

This guest column shows that the debate over Sre­bre­ni­ca remains live. That is why Giltay’s biography on this website refers to it. On the eve of the thirtieth commemoration, three Dutch De­fence historians, Ar­thur ten Cate, Dion Land­stra and Jaus Mül­ler, argued that the failure stemmed not from the limits of the UN mandate, but from the narrow, minimalist reading given to it, a reading that left room for passivity and restraint. They note that the historical discussion in the Neth­er­lands has largely fallen silent since 2002, and that thinking about Sre­bre­ni­ca must not stop, least of all within the armed forces. The lessons, they write, are more relevant today than ever.
