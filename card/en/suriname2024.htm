Dagblad Suriname on censorship, 13 Feb­ru­ary 2024

The passage

“The book De doof­pot­ge­ne­raal by Edwin Giltay was placed under a distribution ban in 2015 because a former employee of the Military In­tel­li­gence Service claimed she had been described in it incorrectly. On appeal this ban was overturned. The Hague Court of Appeal stated that the book finds sufficient support in the facts and contributes to the public debate on Sre­bre­ni­ca.” The paragraph on censorship in democratic countries; the ban on the book is the third of the three cases the paper lists there. Trans­lat­ed from the Dutch; the original wording is below. De doof­pot­ge­ne­raal is the book’s original Dutch title; the English edition is The Cover-up Gen­eral.

Original wording

“Het boek De doof­pot­ge­ne­raal van Edwin Giltay kreeg in 2015 een verspreidingsverbod opgelegd omdat een voormalige medewerkster van de Militaire Inlichtingendienst claimde dat ze er onjuist in was beschreven. In hoger beroep werd dit verbod vernietigd. Het Gerechtshof Den Haag gaf aan dat het boek voldoende steun vindt in de feiten en een bijdrage levert aan het maatschappelijk debat over Srebrenica.” Quoted from the editorial as published on dbsuriname.com. These are the last three sentences of that paragraph; the sentences before them concern the two Belgian cases. The paper prints the passage without quotation marks of its own.

Why this document matters

In this editorial of 13 February 2024 on police states, censorship and self-censorship, Dag­blad Su­ri­name, a Su­ri­nam­ese daily, sets the 2015 distribution ban on the book beside two Belgian cases as examples showing that books can be banned in a democracy too. It reports that the ban was overturned on appeal and that the Court of Appeal of The Hague stated that the book finds sufficient support in the facts and contributes to the public debate on Sre­bre­ni­ca. The judgment itself says that the book’s description of the former employee ‘at least partly finds support in the facts’ (para. 4.10) and that the inaccuracies are insufficient to call the author’s care into question (para. 4.9). The paper adds that in this case freedom of expression must outweigh the right of the former employee (Barbara Overduyn, whom the paper does not name) to protection of her honour and reputation. The essay lists this piece among the international coverage around the book’s launch abroad in 2024.
