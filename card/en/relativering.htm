National Ombudsman to Giltay, 11 Feb­ru­ary 2000

The passage

“Finally, I would point out in general terms that in cases such as this, where the assertions of a number of those involved are diametrically opposed, it is often not possible to speak of ‘the truth’. It is not the case that the National Ombudsman regards the account of the former MID employee concerned as absolute truth, and your account thereby as untrue. This is also clear from the wording of consideration II.7, in which the word ‘plausible’ is used several times.” Page 2, the closing substantive paragraph of the letter. Translated from the Dutch; the original wording is below.

Original wording

“Ten slotte wil ik er in algemene zin nog op wijzen dat het in zaken als deze, waarin beweringen van een aantal betrokkenen haaks op elkaar staan, vaak niet mogelijk is om te spreken van ‘de waarheid’. Het is niet zo dat de Nationale ombudsman de lezing van de betrokken ex-medewerkster van de MID als absolute waarheid ziet, en uw lezing daarmee als onwaar. Dit blijkt ook duidelijk uit de formulering van overweging II.7., waarin verschillende malen het woord ‘aannemelijk’ gebruikt wordt.” The letter itself sets ‘de waarheid’ and ‘aannemelijk’ in straight double quotation marks; they appear here as single quotation marks because the whole passage is already quoted. The Dutch word ‘ex-medewerkster’ is feminine; the English rendering follows the essay, which reads ‘former MID employee’.

Why this document matters

In June 2017 the Ministry of De­fence refused to retract the 1999 ministerial declaration of insanity, basing that refusal in part on the Om­buds­man report of the same year. This letter, written less than two months after that report appeared, shows that the Om­buds­man himself did not regard his own findings as a verdict on who was telling the truth. He points out that where the assertions of those involved are diametrically opposed it is often not possible to speak of ‘the truth’. He therefore does not regard the former employee’s account as absolute truth. This, he says, is also clear from consideration II.7 of his report, which uses the word ‘plausible’ several times. That consideration, however, concerns an entirely different point: whether the MID had introduced incriminating information about Giltay into the selection procedure. It says nothing about the former employee’s characterisation of Giltay.
