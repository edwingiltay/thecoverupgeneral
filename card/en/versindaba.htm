Gerard Scharn on The Cover-up Gen­er­al in Versindaba, 5 August 2024

The passage

“De doofpotgeneraal, gov­ern­ment es­pi­o­nage on the shop floor, by Edwin Giltay, first published by Speakeasy in 2014, takes the reader from one as­ton­ish­ment to the next. In 1998 the author was working as a help­desk employee at the cable operator Casema, where he became caught up against his will in a power struggle within military in­tel­li­gence. Why does a general have a private spy in­fil­trate the shop floor, and what is the ultimate purpose of these murky prac­tices. The author is ap­proached to come and work as an in­tel­li­gence analyst. He reveals matters that cannot bear the light of day.” The first paragraph on the book and the first four sentences of the next, run together. Translated from the Dutch; the original wording is below. De doof­pot­ge­ne­raal is the book’s original Dutch title; the English edition is The Cover-up Gen­eral.

Original wording

“De doofpotgeneraal – overheidsspionage op de bedrijfsvloer – van Edwin Giltay waarvan de eerste druk werd uitgegeven door Speakeasy in 2014 laat de lezer van de ene verbazing in de ander vallen. De schrijver werkte in 1998 als helpdeskmedewerker bij kabelexploitant Casema waar hij ongewild betrokken raakt bij een machtsconflict binnen het militaire inlichtingenwezen. Waarom laat een generaal een privé spion op de werkvloer infiltreren en wat is het uiteindelijke doel van deze duistere praktijken. De schrijver wordt benaderd om aan de slag te gaan als inlichtingenanalist. Hij onthult zaken die het daglicht niet kunnen verdragen.” The piece is written in Dutch; Versindaba is in Afrikaans, but also publishes Dutch and Flemish contributions. The question in the third sentence ends without a question mark, and ‘privé spion’ is written as two words, both as printed.

Why this document matters

This piece appeared on a South African literary weblog, under the heading ‘Merkwaardige boeken’ (Remarkable books). The writer Gerard Scharn sets out what the book is about, a general who has a private spy in­fil­trate a work­place, and adds that the author reveals matters that cannot bear the light of day. He goes on to note that the attempts to ban the book did not succeed, and that a few months earlier it had been taken into the col­lec­tion of the Banned Books Museum in Tallinn. The essay’s timeline lists this piece under 2024, International launch.
