Court of Ap­peal of The Hague, judgment of 27 June 2017 (ECLI:­NL:­GHDHA:­2017:­1761)

The passage

“rules that the State acted wrongfully I. by facilitating the separation of the male refugees by the Bosnian Serbs on 13 July 1995 by letting the refugees go to the buses in groups and through ‘the corridor’, and II. by not offering the male refugees who were inside the compound on 13 July 1995 the choice to stay within the compound, thereby denying them the 30% chance of not being exposed to inhumane treatment and executions by the Bosnian Serbs;” The operative part of the judgment, under the heading Ruling (Dutch: Beslissing), which applies in both cases and in the principal appeal as well as the cross-appeal. According to the court, some 350 male refugees were on the compound (para. 42.2). The wording is the court’s own official English translation, published separately as ECLI:NL:GHDHA:2017:3376; the Dutch original is below. The Court of Appeal first set aside the District Court judgment of 16 July 2014. The reasoning behind the 30 per cent is in paras 68 and 69.1.

Original wording

“verklaart voor recht dat de Staat onrechtmatig heeft gehandeld I. door op 13 juli 1995 de afscheiding van de mannelijke vluchtelingen door de Bosnische Serven te vergemakkelijken door de vluchtelingen in groepen en door ‘de sluis’ naar de bussen te laten gaan, en II. door de mannelijke vluchtelingen die zich op 13 juli 1995 op de compound bevonden niet de keus te bieden om op de compound te blijven en hun aldus de kans van 30% te onthouden om niet te worden blootgesteld aan de onmenselijke behandelingen en executies door de Bosnische Serven;” In the source, declarations I and II appear on two lines; they are joined here with a space. ‘De sluis’, literally the sluice, is rendered as ‘the corridor’ in the court’s own English translation.

Why this document matters

This is the judgment in which the Court of Appeal of The Hague put the State’s liability in the Mothers of Srebrenica case at thirty per cent. The court held that Dutchbat had made it easier for the Bosnian Serbs to separate the male refugees. According to the court, Dutchbat had also failed to offer the roughly 350 men on the compound the choice of staying there, and had thereby denied them a thirty per cent chance of escaping inhumane treatment and execution. Two years later the Supreme Court reduced that share to ten per cent.

In their statement of appeal in these same proceedings, the relatives’ lawyers cited The Cover-up General, referring to four pages of the book for the claim that Dutchbat officer Johannes Rutten’s film roll had not failed and that the photos have been withheld ever since.
